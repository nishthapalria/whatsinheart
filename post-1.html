Entry No. 1

Slow Mornings, Defended

An unfashionable case for doing nothing in particular before nine.

Somewhere along the way it became embarrassing to admit you don't do anything useful before nine in the morning. Everyone is journaling, or lifting something heavy, or drinking a green liquid with a name that sounds like a spell. I get up, and I sit, and I look at the wall for a while, and I consider this a full and complete use of the hour.

I used to feel guilty about it. I'd scroll through other people's mornings — the cold plunge, the five pages, the perfectly organized desk — and feel like I was failing some test I never signed up to take. But the wall-looking has started to feel less like idleness and more like maintenance. The mind needs a little static before the signal starts.

There's a particular kind of thought that only shows up when nothing is being asked of you. Not the productive kind. The kind that arrives sideways — a memory of a smell, an unfinished argument from a decade ago, the sudden correct word for something you'd been describing badly for weeks. You can't summon these on command. You can only leave the door open.

So that's the whole defense: not that slowness is more virtuous than speed, just that it does something speed can't. Some mornings I still rush. But the ones I protect — the ones where I let the kettle boil longer than it needs to, just to have a reason to stand still — those are the mornings I remember.
